Treasuries for the next 12 months: SGOV, SHY, IEF or TLT

The question: which of four Treasury funds to hold for the next 12 months. SGOV holds Treasury bills that mature within 3 months, so it behaves like cash. SHY, IEF and TLT hold longer bonds, which pay more interest but lose value when interest rates rise. The brief works out what each fund would return under six possible futures, in four steps:

  1. Find what the market already expects the Fed to do. Bond prices contain that forecast (section 1).
  2. Describe each future as a surprise against that forecast, using what happened in similar past episodes or a formula for how the Fed sets rates (sections 4 and 5).
  3. Turn each future into the interest rate every bond would pay 12 months from now.
  4. Reprice each fund at those rates to get its 12-month return. Section 2 checks this step against what the funds actually returned this year.

"Points" means percentage points: a 0.25-point hike takes a 3.75% rate to 4.00%. A bond's yield is the yearly return from buying it today and holding it until it matures. Every other term is explained where it first appears and listed in Terms at the bottom.

1. What the market expects the Fed to do

A 2-year Treasury pays about what you would earn by buying 3-month bills and reinvesting them for two years. So its yield tells you where investors think short-term rates, which the Fed sets, are headed. Longer bonds also pay a little extra on top of that forecast, called the term premium, for locking money up longer and taking bigger price swings.

To split a yield into the forecast and the term premium, the brief uses a model the New York Fed publishes every day, called ACM after its authors (Adrian, Crump and Moench). On Oct 22 it says investors expect the Fed's rate to average over the next 12 months and over the 12 months after that. The Fed's rate today is 3.88%, so the market expects about 2.4 more quarter-point hikes by late 2027, then a slow drift down to 4.12% ten years out.

What is left over is the term premium: . That is the extra a longer bond earns over bills if the market's forecast turns out right. Models disagree on this split: the San Francisco Fed's model puts the 10-year term premium at 1.35, about double ACM's.

2. The four funds

Each fund holds Treasuries in a different maturity range. Duration measures how sensitive a fund's price is to rates: with a duration of 6.83, a 1-point rise in yields cuts the price about 6.83%, and a 1-point fall raises it about as much. The interest keeps coming either way.

If the market's forecast comes true, the longer funds beat SGOV by 0.54 to 1.25 points over the year. That gap is the term premium at work: pay for taking rate risk. Every scenario in section 5 starts from this baseline and adds the effect of rates ending somewhere other than the forecast.

Checking the return math against real returns

To compute a fund's return in any scenario, the brief treats each fund as a ladder: a set of bonds that mature at evenly spaced dates. SHY is modeled as one bond maturing each month from 1 to 2.8 years out, IEF from 7 to 10 years and TLT from 20 to 30 years. Each month the model reprices every bond at that month's rates, collects the interest and swaps bonds that have aged out of the range for new ones, which is what the real funds do. TLT's bonds get a 3.5% coupon so the ladder has the same rate sensitivity as the real fund.

4. How the Fed sets its rate: a formula fitted to history

To say what the Fed does in each scenario, the brief needs a link from the economy to the Fed's rate. Economists use the Taylor rule for this: the Fed raises its rate when inflation runs above its 2% target and cuts when unemployment rises. Fitting that kind of formula to the Fed's actual decisions from 1988 to 2026 (leaving out years when the rate was stuck near zero) gives:

r* is the neutral rate after inflation: the rate that neither speeds up nor slows the economy. The brief uses the New York Fed's Laubach-Williams estimate, 1.65% today. The second line says the Fed moves gradually: each quarter it closes 6% of the gap between its current rate and the formula's target.

It falls furthest short in recessions, because the Fed cuts on forecasts and market stress before unemployment has risen much. What it says today

Today the formula's target is 7.5%, because core PCE is a full point above 2% and unemployment has fallen 0.2 points over the year. Since the Fed moves gradually, that turns into steady hikes rather than a jump: 4.37% by October 2027 if core PCE falls to the Fed's projected 2.5%. That matches the market's forecast. Each 0.5 points that core PCE runs above or below that path moves the October 2027 rate by about 0.15 points in the same direction.

5. Six scenarios

Each scenario is a story about the next 12 months. The brief describes each story as a surprise against the market's forecast from section 1, then turns it into interest rates two ways:

  • Historical analogs: find past episodes that fit the story and measure how far rates ended up from what the market had forecast at the start. In the four soft landings since 1995, for example, the 2-year yield ended the year 0.98 points below the forecast on average. The brief applies the same surprise to today's forecast.
  • Structural model: run the story's changes in inflation and unemployment through the Fed formula from section 4 to get the Fed's rate for the next year. Long-term rates then move by the share of a Fed surprise that markets have carried into their long-term forecasts in the past (0.63 for the 10-year, per ACM), plus any change in the term premium.

6. Does the model work on past episodes?

For each past episode, the structural model was given what actually happened to inflation, unemployment, r* and the term premium, plus the market's forecast at the start. It then predicted the Fed's rate and yields a year later. The table compares its predictions with what happened.

EpisodeFed rate2-year10-year30-year

Each cell: predicted / actual change over 12 months, in points. Typical miss is the root-mean-square error, a standard measure of the size of a typical error. "Predict no change" is the miss you get by assuming rates stay put, a baseline any model should beat. A correlation of 1 would mean predictions moved exactly with reality.

Where it fails: long-term rates in big cycles. In 1994 it predicted the 30-year yield would stay flat, and the 30-year rose 1.24 points, because investors came to expect high rates to last for years, longer than the model assumes. In recessions it predicts long-term rates that are too high. The analog method avoids this kind of mistake, but each scenario rests on only two to four past episodes, and the ranges under each return in section 5 show how much those episodes disagree.

10. Method and limits

  • Priced path: ACM risk-neutral yields for 1 to 10 years, converted to 1-year forward rates and held flat beyond 10 years. Scenario surprises are added to the forward-implied change in each yield over the year.
  • Fed rule: inertial Taylor rule with the Laubach-Williams r*, estimated by grid search over the smoothing parameter and least squares for the rest. Unemployment enters as its 4-quarter change. A version that adds the unemployment level fits slightly better (dynamic error 0.83) but gives the level the wrong sign, so it is not used.
  • Analogs: 12-month windows starting at the last hike (soft landings), the first hike of a fast cycle, the market peak before a recession and the start of the 1990s productivity boom. Term-premium shocks use 4- and 5-month windows, the full length of those episodes. Covid is left out of the bust average because the shock did not come from the economy.
  • AI boom: the 1990s analog raised Laubach-Williams r* by 0.31. Larger booms add (1.07 × trend growth − 0.31) of neutral-rate shift through the structural model, using the Laubach-Williams estimate that r* rises 1.07 per point of trend growth.
  • Returns: yields move in a straight line from today to the 12-month curve; funds are monthly-rebalanced ladders; SGOV earns the average of 1- and 3-month yields less 0.10 and its fee. Returns are before taxes and trading costs.
  • Not modeled: changes in fund holdings beyond the ladder, the bid-ask spread and correlation with your other holdings.

Terms

Yield
The yearly return from buying a bond today and holding it to maturity. When yields rise, existing bonds fall in price.
Points
Percentage points. A rate going from 4.00% to 4.25% rose 0.25 points.
Duration
How much a bond fund's price moves when yields move. Duration 6.83: yields up 1 point, price down about 6.83%.
Market forecast, priced path
The path of the Fed's rate that today's bond prices imply.
Term premium
The extra yield a longer bond pays beyond the market's forecast of short rates, as pay for tying money up longer.
ACM model
A model from the New York Fed, named after Adrian, Crump and Moench, that splits each Treasury yield into the market's forecast and the term premium. Published daily.
Ladder
A set of bonds maturing at evenly spaced dates. The brief models each fund this way to compute its returns.
Taylor rule
A formula for the Fed's rate based on inflation and unemployment. Section 4 fits one to the Fed's decisions since 1988.
r* (neutral rate)
The rate after inflation that neither speeds up nor slows the economy. The Laubach-Williams model at the New York Fed estimates it at 1.65%.
Core PCE, core CPI
Two measures of consumer prices excluding food and energy. The Fed targets 2% on PCE.
Historical analog
A past episode that matches a scenario, used to measure how far rates ended up from the market's forecast.
Root-mean-square error, R², standard error
Typical size of a model's miss; share of movement a relationship explains, from 0 to 1; uncertainty in an estimate.
