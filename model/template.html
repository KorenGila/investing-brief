1. What the market prices

The NY Fed's ACM model splits each Treasury yield into the expected path of the short rate and a term premium. On Oct 22 the expected short rate averages over the next year and over the year after, against a 3.88% fed funds rate today. That is 2.4 quarter-point hikes by late 2027, followed by a slow decline to 4.12% ten years out.

Subtracting the expected path from each par yield leaves term premiums of . Those premiums are what longer bonds earn over bills if the expected path comes true. The San Francisco Fed's model puts the 10-year premium at 1.35, about double, so the split between expectations and premium carries about 0.7 points of model uncertainty at 10 years.

The rule gets the direction of the four large moves right and reaches 41% to 86% of their size. It understates recession cuts most, because the Fed cuts on forecasts and financial stress before unemployment has risen much. It missed both recent pauses: it called a hike in 2019 and a cut in 2024.

What it says today

With Laubach-Williams r* at 1.65, core PCE a point above 2% and unemployment down 0.2 over the year, the rule's target is 7.5%. Smoothing turns that into steady hikes: 4.37% by October 2027 if core PCE glides to the Fed's 2.5% projection, which averages to the market's path. Each 0.5-point miss in core PCE against that glide moves the rule's October 2027 rate by about 0.15 points in the same direction, and the 2-year by more, because the market extends the miss into later years.

5. Six scenarios

Each scenario is a surprise relative to the priced path, turned into yields two ways:

  • Historical analogs: the average of what happened in matching episodes, measured against the forward curve at the time. A 2-year surprise of −1.0 means the 2-year ended the year 1.0 point below where forwards had pointed.
  • Structural model: the scenario's surprises in core PCE, unemployment and r* run through the Fed rule for a year. After that, the Fed's deviation from the priced path fades at 13% a year, the rate that matches ACM's finding that 10-year expectations move 0.63 for each point of 2-year expectations. Term-premium changes enter through ACM's maturity loadings.

6. Model checks

Structural model backtest, 12-month changes

For each episode the model gets the actual changes in core PCE, unemployment, r* and the ACM term premium, plus the forward curve at the start. It then predicts the Fed rate and yields a year later:

EpisodeFed rate2-year10-year30-year

Cells show predicted / actual change in points. RMSE is the root mean squared error across episodes; "no change" is the error from predicting zero change.

The model's weak spot is the long end in big cycles. In 1994 it predicted a flat 30-year, and the 30-year rose 1.24 points: ACM attributes that rise to expectations that stayed high for years, longer than the model's 13% decay allows. In busts it overstates long yields because term premiums jumped (1.6 points in 2001) while expectations fell further than the model lets them. The analog method has no mapping error, but it rests on two to four episodes per scenario, and the ranges under each return show how much those episodes disagree.

10. Method and limits

  • Priced path: ACM risk-neutral yields for 1 to 10 years, converted to 1-year forward rates and held flat beyond 10 years. Scenario surprises are added to the forward-implied change in each yield over the year.
  • Fed rule: inertial Taylor rule with the Laubach-Williams r*, estimated by grid search over the smoothing parameter and least squares for the rest. Unemployment enters as its 4-quarter change. A version that adds the unemployment level fits slightly better (dynamic error 0.83) but gives the level the wrong sign, so it is not used.
  • Analogs: 12-month windows starting at the last hike (soft landings), the first hike of a fast cycle, the market peak before a recession and the start of the 1990s productivity boom. Term-premium shocks use 4- and 5-month windows, the full length of those episodes. Covid is left out of the bust average because the shock did not come from the economy.
  • AI boom: the 1990s analog raised Laubach-Williams r* by 0.31. Larger booms add (1.07 × trend growth − 0.31) of neutral-rate shift through the structural model, using the Laubach-Williams estimate that r* rises 1.07 per point of trend growth.
  • Returns: yields move in a straight line from today to the 12-month curve; funds are monthly-rebalanced ladders; SGOV earns the average of 1- and 3-month yields less 0.10 and its fee. Returns are before taxes and trading costs.
  • Not modeled: changes in fund holdings beyond the ladder, the bid-ask spread and correlation with your other holdings.
