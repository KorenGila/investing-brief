Treasuries for the next 12 months: SGOV, SHY, IEF or TLT

The question: which of four Treasury funds to hold for the next 12 months. SGOV holds Treasury bills that mature within 3 months, so it behaves like cash. SHY, IEF and TLT hold longer bonds, which pay more interest but lose value when interest rates rise. The brief works it out in three steps:

  1. Take the bond market's own forecast of interest rates as the center (section 1).
  2. Measure how far that forecast has missed over every 12-month period since 1990, and use those misses as the range of what can happen (section 4).
  3. Turn each possible outcome into each fund's return with a one-line formula checked against this year's real returns (section 2). Two optional knobs let you move the center to your own view (section 5).

"Points" means percentage points: a 0.25-point hike takes a 3.75% rate to 4.00%. A bond's yield is the yearly return from buying it today and holding it until it matures. Other terms are explained where they first appear and listed in Terms.

1. What the market expects

A 2-year Treasury pays about what you would earn by buying 3-month bills and reinvesting them for two years. So its yield tells you where investors think short-term rates, which the Fed sets, are headed. Longer bonds also pay a little extra on top of that forecast, called the term premium, for locking money up longer and taking bigger price swings.

To split a yield into the forecast and the term premium, the brief uses a model the New York Fed publishes every day, called ACM after its authors (Adrian, Crump and Moench). On Oct 22 it says investors expect the Fed's rate to average over the next 12 months and over the 12 months after that. The Fed's rate today is 3.88%, so the market expects about 2.4 more quarter-point hikes by late 2027, then a slow drift down to 4.12% ten years out.

What is left over is the term premium: . Models disagree on this split: the San Francisco Fed's model puts the 10-year term premium at 1.35, about double ACM's.

2. The four funds and the return formula

FundHoldsYieldYearly feeDurationConvexity12-month return if the forecast is rightGain over SGOV

Yield, duration and convexity are as published by iShares for Oct 22.

Every return on this page comes from one formula:

12-month return ≈ average yield over the year − fee − duration × (change in yield) + ½ × convexity × (change in yield)²
  • The first part is the interest you collect. If yields move during the year, the fund reinvests at the new rates, so the formula uses the average of the starting and ending yield.
  • The second part is the price change. Duration 6.84 means a 1-point rise in yields cuts the price about 6.84%, and a 1-point fall raises it about as much.
  • The third part, convexity, corrects for the fact that prices fall a little less than duration predicts when yields rise, and rise a little more when they fall. It only matters for big moves in long bonds: for TLT a 1-point move in either direction adds 1.6 points.
  • "Change in yield" is the change in the Treasury yield at the fund's average maturity: 0.12 years for SGOV, 1.89 for SHY, 8.41 for IEF and 26.03 for TLT.

Check against what the funds returned in 2026

The formula runs slightly high for all four funds, and most for TLT, by 0.39 points. TLT's bonds span 20 to 30 years. This year 20-year yields rose 0.66 points and 30-year yields 0.56, and the formula uses a single maturity of 26 years, which misses that difference. The formula also treats duration as fixed, while a fund's duration drifts as yields move. Both errors grow with the size of the move, so in the largest outcomes in section 5, read TLT's numbers as accurate to about 1 point.

4. How wrong the forecast has been

For every month from January 1990 to October 2025, the brief measured how far yields ended up a year later from that month's forecast. That gives one-year forecast misses at each fund's maturity. Two facts come out of them:

  • The forecast ran too high on average, by points at SHY's maturity, because rates fell for most of 1990-2020. The brief subtracts that average, so the misses describe how far off the forecast can be without assuming which way.
  • The misses are large. In 90% of years the 2-year yield ended between and points from the forecast. Long yields miss by less: TLT's 26-year maturity between and .

Each point is the miss for the 12 months starting that month, before removing the average. The big ones line up with the 1994 and 2022 hiking cycles and the 2001, 2008 and 2020 cuts.

Misses move together, mostly. When the 2-year missed by 1 point, IEF's yield moved and TLT's in the same direction on average. Applying each month's misses together keeps that link, and the occasional years when short and long yields went opposite ways.

The months overlap: a window starting in January and one starting in February share 11 months. So the 430 windows hold about 36 independent years.

Recessions

Four recessions started between January 1990 and September 2026, a rate of 0.11 a year. In the two that followed stock market busts, 2001 and 2008, the 2-year ended 2.35 and 1.79 points below the forecast, the scale to use on the Fed-path knob for a recession.

9. Method and limits

  • Center: ACM's expected short-rate path on Oct 22, held flat after 10 years. Each fund's forecast yield change over the year comes from that path at the fund's average maturity.
  • Spread: for each month-end from January 1990 to October 2025, the miss at each fund's average maturity: the yield 12 months later minus the yield then, minus the forecast change then. The 1990-2025 average miss is subtracted. The 30-year was not issued from 2002 to 2006, so those months use the 20-year.
  • Knobs: a Fed-path view shifts each fund's yield by its historical slope on the 2-year miss. A term-premium view shifts each yield by ACM's term premium at that maturity per point of the 10-year premium (0.89 for the 30-year, from a regression of the 30-year yield on the 10-year premium). Views move the center only.
  • Returns: the formula in section 2 with iShares' published yield, duration and convexity. Before taxes and trading costs.
  • What this assumes: the market's forecast is the best center, and the next year's miss is drawn from the same range as the last 35 years'. It ignores today's level of rates and of the term premium when sizing that range. Overlapping windows mean about 36 independent years.

Terms

Yield
The yearly return from buying a bond today and holding it to maturity. When yields rise, existing bonds fall in price.
Points
Percentage points. A rate going from 4.00% to 4.25% rose 0.25 points.
Duration
How much a bond fund's price moves when yields move. Duration 6.84: yields up 1 point, price down about 6.84%. It is also the average time until the fund's payments arrive, weighted by their value today.
Convexity
The correction to duration for big moves. Prices fall a little less than duration says when yields rise, and rise a little more when they fall.
Market forecast
The path of the Fed's rate that today's bond prices imply.
Term premium
The extra yield a longer bond pays beyond the forecast of short rates, as pay for tying money up longer.
ACM model
A New York Fed model, named after Adrian, Crump and Moench, that splits each Treasury yield into the market's forecast and the term premium. Published daily, with history back to 1961.
Core PCE, core CPI
Two measures of consumer prices excluding food and energy. The Fed targets 2% on PCE.
Forecast miss
How far a yield ended up, 12 months later, from what the market had forecast at the start.
Percentile
The 90th percentile is the value that 90% of past observations fall below. The 5th to 95th percentile range holds 90% of them.
